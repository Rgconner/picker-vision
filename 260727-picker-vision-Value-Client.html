Every great demo has an origin story. Ours started with a question so mundane it barely seemed worth asking — and ended with a fully deployed retail shop running on a Raspberry Pi.

Chapter 1 — The Question

It started with a barcode and a scanner that wasn't quite good enough

The problem was simple, the kind warehouses deal with every day: pickers — the people who walk the floor pulling items for orders — spend a surprising amount of time second-guessing themselves. Is that the right barcode? Is that the right shelf? Did I already scan that?

Existing handheld scanners are fine for what they are. But they're not intelligent. They don't know what order you're working. They don't see the tote you're loading. They can't confirm you've picked the right thing in the right quantity.

"What if the camera could help? What if the system could watch the picker work and give real-time guidance — without requiring expensive hardware or a three-year ERP integration project?"

That was the question. It seemed like a reasonable afternoon's worth of exploration.

Chapter 2 — The Experiment

A Raspberry Pi, an IBM Architect, and Bob AI walk into a warehouse

The experiment started with a $70 Raspberry Pi 5 and an IBM architect who wanted to test something. Not a formal project. Not a funded initiative. Just: what can we build together, Bob?

Bob is IBM's AI development assistant — built on watsonx. You describe what you want. Bob writes the code, reasons through the design, catches the bugs, and keeps the architecture coherent across dozens of files simultaneously. The architect provides the domain expertise, the customer context, and the judgment. Bob provides the velocity.

What got built on Day 1: A complete Python computer vision pipeline running on the Pi — dual-engine barcode detection (QR + 1D), staging area detection using Canny edge analysis, MJPEG live video streaming, a FastAPI control surface, and an offline-resilient event publisher that buffers to JSONL when the server is unreachable. Four server-side services. A React web UI. Kubernetes manifests for the whole thing. 50 files. 4,632 lines of code. One commit.

It was, not to put too fine a point on it, a lot.

Chapter 3 — Off-Script

Then someone said: "What if we actually showed this to a customer?"

A working system is impressive. A working system with a story is a demo. And demos need a scenario that a customer can actually see themselves in.

Abstract warehouses are hard to care about. But a toy shop — a real shop with named products, shelf locations, pick orders, and a tote that needs to be packed just right — that's something you can stand in front of and immediately understand.

"Let's build a demo retail shop. Something small, charming, and slightly absurd. Let's call it Bob's Tiny Treasures."

And this is where the experiment went delightfully off-script.

Because what should have been "add some seed data and a nice label" became: a full warehouse grid, 9 SKUs, 3 pick zones, 9 shelf locations, 3 live orders, a bin-packing engine that figures out how to stack items into a tote, a multi-step Pack Wizard for the picker to step through, a server-side PDF label sheet generator with Avery-compatible layout, stock location labels, a brand logo, and a Kubernetes namespace overlay — all fully deployed alongside the production system.

Bob's Tiny Treasures — what's on the shelves:
  • Plush robots (small, medium, large)
  • Miniature circuit boards (decorative, naturally)
  • Holographic sticker packs
  • Retro LED keychains
  • Binary-patterned socks
  • Tiny rubber ducks wearing graduation caps
  • A "World's Smallest Mainframe" novelty paperweight

Every product is real enough to pick, scan, pack, and ship. The warehouse knows where each one lives. The system knows how to fit them in a tote. The labels know which shelf they came from.

Chapter 4 — What This Actually Demonstrates

This isn't a prototype. It's a proof of what AI-assisted delivery can do.

The system running behind Bob's Tiny Treasures is not a mockup. It's production code running in Kubernetes, responding to real barcode scans, streaming real video from a real camera, and publishing real events over a real WebSocket feed.

A traditional development project of this scope — 45 work items across platform services, mobile AR, and a full demo scenario — would take a 7-person team approximately 9.5 weeks to deliver, at a cost in the mid-six figures.

This was delivered in 4 calendar days. 12.5 active architect hours. The architect brought the domain expertise, the customer context, and the vision of what the demo needed to be. Bob turned every conversation into working, deployable code.

"Every dollar invested in this demo returned 74 dollars in equivalent professional value — against industry benchmarks for a comparably-staffed development team."

That's not the headline, though. The headline is what this demo shows a customer standing in front of it: that the gap between "I have a warehouse problem" and "I have a working AI system that helps my pickers" is not years and millions of dollars. It's a conversation with the right partner and the right tools.
